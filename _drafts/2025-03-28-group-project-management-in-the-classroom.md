So in my own courses in the library, or my own fellowships in the library, it's very common for us, at a certain point to pivot to just group time where this where in the students decide what we need to work on on a day to day basis, and the bulk of the time is just spent setting an agenda and then executing that agenda. So I'm was planning on doing the same thing again, but to provide a little bit of structure for that in undergraduate curricular context, what I've planned on doing is implementing a series of things from this previous blog post that I co wrote with Rhonda Grizzle, our expert project training software training specialist in the scholars lab on a meeting toolkit for new facilitators. So everything I know about project management comes from Rhonda, and I'm trying to implement some of her lessons here. The primary lesson, the primary deliverable that I'm offering both for as a skill that the students can take away, but also as a way for us to manage our time in the class is the tool known as scrumming. So if you're not familiar, the term scrum comes from rugby, and it's refers to when all the rugby players lock arm over the ball and try to gain possession of the ball and move it forward across the field. It also is a term that has been implemented in software development, particularly within the Agile development framework, and typically refers to a moderated set of practices that you use to get everyone up to speed on where the project is at any given moment. So typically, a scrum will occur like each day. At the beginning of the day, you'll have a few different questions you answer. It's extremely time constrained such that you are getting things on the table for your group without spending a lot of time getting into the weeds. And it's a way of trying to give project updates and constrain those so that you can actually have work time. Because if you've ever gone into a meeting where you're like, Okay, let's give updates on where we're at. You only have an hour to go. You can spend easily 40 to 50 minutes, especially with humanities scholars who like to talk just talking about where you're at, and you never actually get to the point of doing any work. So it's very good for actually making room for work time. So the way that I run scrums, based on my training with Rhonda is that each person goes around and gives one minute describing what they have done, what they need from somebody else and what is next for them. That's it, a whole minute for all three of those things. The first time you Scrum, it's almost always the case that people are going to run out of time, but it teaches people to very succinctly give project updates such that you have a sense of what the next state of work will be, state of work for the project, and what the next steps are. So I wanted to implement this in my class as a way to provide some structure for the work that we are doing, to bring people into the room and let them know, like, yes, we are actually working even though, you know, you could look at the calendar and think, Oh, this is just free time. And then also to help them figure out what how they're going to use that time with each other. But I also wanted to use the time for the different groups to share what they were working on with each other, what kinds of lessons they were learning, what kinds of problems they were running into, such that it wasn't just about their own project, but also a way for sharing that what they were doing with a larger group. So what I settled upon was a sort of modified scrumming practice, wherein we had both an individual Scrum and then a group Scrum. So within the students groups, we start out each class by going around and giving a daily scrum. So each student in the group will have one minute in which they will say, answer three questions, what am I working on? What's next for me and what do I need from somebody else? We'll go through those that's three to four minutes, three to five minutes, depending on the group sizes, and then we'll do a groups from where each group has two to three minutes for answering those same questions in front of the whole class. So it's less about individual tasks and more about the group agenda that they are working on as a way to sharing out the particulars of their own projects with the larger class, because the kinds of projects that people are working on vary broadly, everything from video games data to food and recipe data to museum Data to macroeconomic political data. So each group has very different kinds of questions and problems they're dealing with. And I really hope that they'll all learn from each other to some degree. And I hope that that'll happen in process, as opposed to just at the end, when the students are giving final presentations. But with these daily scrums, I found them very useful for helping to bring people into the room, set the agenda, give a clear delineation between the time outside the class the time in the class, and then also give students a chance to catch up on where they are, where they're at, and set deliverables for what's next. So I think I'm definitely going to keep it incorporated in my group work in the future, but I especially just like this as a way to try and shift my own practices that I use when I have everyone working on one group project versus a range of people working on a range of projects. It's a pretty good daily project management task. Do.
