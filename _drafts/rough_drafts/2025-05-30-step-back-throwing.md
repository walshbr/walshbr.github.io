I had a very short

and mediocre career as an a little league

baseball player.

I got think if I, if memory serves, I got hit with the ball once, and that was it. I was afraid of pitches forever, and I quickly lost interest in playing due to a fear of bodily harm. But one thing I do remember is a particular exercise that we used to do for throwing. I remember doing this with my dad many afternoons growing up when I wasn't trying to convince them to let me go inside and play video games. The idea is you start fairly close together. You throw the ball to the other person. Requires two people. You start fairly close together. You throw a ball to the other person, and you take a step back, if it's a successful catch. And every time you successfully catch the ball, you take another step back, such that you're getting farther and farther apart, and then if you ever drop the ball, you take a step forward. The idea is that you're slowly developing your ability to throw at a great distance, and if you reach the upper limit of your ability to consistently catch and furrow, you'll kind of hover around there and get a lot of practice at exactly the distance that you need to work on. I'm currently listening to small teaching by James and Lang, who uses the metaphor of small ball for teaching and the kind of pedagogy that he's advancing, small ball being all the kinds of baseball moves that can consistently lead to better outcomes, but are not flashy, like bunting, stealing bases, those sorts of things, as opposed to say, having a big slugger come up and get a Grand Slam. The point is, I have baseball on the mind, and I'm thinking about baseball as it relates to teaching. I don't tend to teach writing so much these days, but I'm doing a lot of it. So I'm interested in the kinds of exercises that we might develop for writing for ourselves or for our students. And I thought about how I might adapt this baseball exercise to writing. I'm calling it whatever the name of that baseball exercise is that some critic on it as pertains to writing. So the idea is, you take a particular topic, maybe it's a topic about a book that you're reading or about an article you're writing or whatever you're working on, and you start to write sentences iteratively, starting with a very short length. So you start out with three words, then you write four words, then five words, six words, seven words, different versions of the same sentence. You'll inevitably have to add other ideas to it, but I think you will start to develop a sense of what is within your power and what is not.
