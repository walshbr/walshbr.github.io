One thing that helped me a lot as a graduate student was the Scholars' Lab's willingness to help me prepare for job interviews. I had no idea what to expect, so the practice was hugely beneficial for me—as was the coaching in what they might look like at all. We now regularly advertise these [professional development offerings](https://scholarslab.lib.virginia.edu/professional-development/) so that a student need not know to ask. I thought I would document how we run mock interviews in case the information is useful for others to see how we run things.

## The Process

First, assemble 1-2 others for the committee. Part of the weirdness of interviews is the weirdness of managing a one-sided conversation. It's useful to mirror that for students. Since interview—and, accordingly, mock interview—requests come up very last minute, it's helpful to know who in your community might be interested in serving in this role. I often find people are very happy to accommodate these last-minute requests once they have done them once—we are always happy for our students to succeed! I also try to select people likely to be familiar with the kind of job in question.

One you have the group together, share the following documents with them ahead of time:
 
* the position description
* any job materials the student has
* the plan (this post or otherwise)
* the questions to be asked

They'll want to familiarize themselves with all the relevant materials, though since things are often last minute you might just expect to give some of this information verbally when you meet.

Schedule 90 minutes for the mock interview, though 60 minutes will work if necessary. I typically use this format:

* 10 minutes to brief on the format and give general interview thoughts.
* 60 minutes for the interview.
* 20 minutes to debrief, give feedback, and discuss.

From there you should be ready to carry out the mock. Share all notes, guides, and questions (including those you didn't ask) after the fact with the students for their own prep work. Follow up with them close to the date and afterwards, both to encourage them and to find out how to better mirror the mock format to what they saw in reality. More information about the specific parts of the session itself below.

## Discussion of the Mock to Come

Since most students come to us without much experience at all interviewing for alt-ac or DH positions, I typically open with just a few minutes discussing interviews in general. I often note that these types of positions are not quite full academic positions, and not quite standard tech jobs. Reviewing the posting ahead of time might help to give some sense of what they can expect, as each position is unique. If the student is interviewing for a postdoc for example, some might be more like pre-faculty fellowships, with a heavy focus on personal research in addition to staff responsibilities. Others might be more flavored as pre-staff positions with little research obligations. Temper expectations and the format accordingly. 

Plan to mirror the format of the interview – phone, Zoom, in-person. It’s important to practice as though it were the real thing. The reason for this is that each is awful in their own ways that can't be anticipated ahead of time. I typically discuss the particular weirdness of the selected format with the student quite frankly. I have seen people's pets defecate in the background of zoom interviews before! It happens. Best not to be thrown but also keep in mind how you can minimize the risks of such things for yourself.

Many formal interviews have HR requirements that require interviewers to ask the same questions of each candidate. This has a lot of ramifications. Committee members might ask follow-up questions, but back and forth conversation is likely to be minimal. They might aggressively be taking notes while you talk. The committee will typically move person by person down the line and each read questions from a prepared list. These facts can have the effect of feeling like you have no real rapport with the people in the room because you get little response visually or verbally to much of what you say. Expect it so that you don't get thrown. All of this is to say: the awkwardness is not you. It is almost always a reflection of the format, where people are trying to figure out who should go when, who should say what, what to do next, etc. 

I usually close by asking the student to take the mock as seriously as they would a real interview, up to and including trying their best to stumble through answers as they would for the real thing. This mostly just means avoiding passing on any question with a response like "well I should probably think about that more." Just do your best - we can discuss later. 

## The Mock Itself

The bulk of the time is spent actually asking questions. I usually offer a few options for the mock committee. These questions can drawn from a experience and made up on the spot if they like, but I also provide a bank of questions based around different topics for my colleagues to use if necessary. During the mock, we alternate who is asking questions to mimic the weird experience of interviewing by committee. And we try to draw questions from across a spectrum of topics. Those broad buckets along with some example questions follow:

* First Question
  * What drew you to this position? Why this place?
* Position-specific Questions:
  *	We are really concerned about X problem local to us. How would you address it?
    * I often research for five minutes and come up with something ahead of time.
  *	We want to get more undergrads involved. How would you do that?
  *	How do you get faculty to collaborate meaningfully with staff?
  *	Do you want to use this as a faculty stepping stone (ideally yes or no depending on the position) - how can we help?
* Research Questions
  *	Describe your research and what you think of as your primary intervention.
  *	How does your dissertation engage in digital humanities?
  *	If you had to construct a through line for your work – dissertation through extra-curricular activities – what would it be?
  *	What is next big project? (might be book? A DH Project)
* Teaching Questions
  *	What is your vision for pedagogy (especially re: DH) and how we might integrate it here? 
  *	How might that be translated to a curriculum or minor?
  *	How does DH inform your approach to teaching?
  *	What DH teaching have you done?
  *	If you were to teach a DH course what would it be?
  *	What kind of support do you need for teaching?
* Community Questions
  *	How do you approach collaboration? (push to talk about both technical and project management strategies)
  *	Putting together a curriculum. What do you think a good DH curriculum should look like?
  *	What experience do you have with grant writing? One problem we have is that when we write grants then the money ends. What do you do about that? How could you have this position help us (and you) grow?
  *	How do you bring students into your program when you’re a multidisciplinary org like ours?
* Technical Questions
  * We are interested in how you would begin to design a digital archive. Talk us through it.
  * **Hand a list of data formatted differently** - What do you see here? Why does this matter? How would you address it?
    * Actual interview question I had once
  * What did you learn from your biggest technical failure?
* Last Question
  * Do you have any questions for us?

I could go on and on. I typically flavor the questions a bit towards the specific job in question. For example, a DH Developer mock might have more technical questions than an interview for a DH Specialist. But I do think giving a broad spectrum of questions, difficulties, and topics can be helpful for students as they try to figure out what they can expect. 

## The Debrief

Perhaps the most helpful piece of the mock is the feedback that students will receive from the committee. Each person will have their own things that they noticed, but I often find that there are a few categories that students might especially need to here advice on. 

Because students often feel like imposters, it can be easy to overwhelm them with feedback. So we often open debrief sessions simply by encouraging them. They survived. They can do this. And be careful to consider—and frame—your advice in the context of the circumstances. If the actual interview is the next day, a student cannot expect to wholesale change their personality based on your feedback—and advice to do so might just make them panic. Instead, emphasize those things that feel doable and learnable in the time allotted. 

One way to do this is to start with the good that you noticed in the mock performance. Were there specific questions they responded well to? Can you help them to extrapolate that example to a more generalized approach to responding? Were there responses where they felt particularly light on their feet? It's easy to focus on the bad, so the students might need your help seeing their strengths. And opening with these moments can offer a healthy frame for the conversation to follow.  

Students often lack confidence in their own experiences and their ability to speak from them to the job at hand. I always encourage students to think about their current identities *as students* as a kind of superpower. Staff and faculty putting together DH programming often have to work hard to reach out to students just like them. They're living it! It's just a matter of reframing their own experiences as expertise and extrapolating a bit from their own circumstances. What has worked for them about their own DH experiences? What has not? What lessons could they take elsewhere? They often know more than they might think!

I could offer much more in the way of specific, common traps that students tend to find: contextualizing themselves as a PhD graduate applying for library work, saying enough for a particular question, recognizing those questions that feel like traps, etc. But really I would just trust yourself and your students. In the same way that your students are capable of shining but might need the help to see it, I am confident that someone who has read this far in a post on this topic will have good instincts about what to share with a student about their interview performance. 

## My Own Caveats

I share with you, Dear Reader, the same caveat that I always share with students: I only have sat on so many kinds of search committees. Those I have served on primarily pertained to digital humanities, alt-ac, or library jobs. Other institutional contexts and types of positions will look different. I'll close with some advice in that regard: know your own limits. When I get a request for something more out of my wheelhouse—like a faculty position or an industry gig—I will try to pull in folks with experience in those contexts. Your university might also a career center that, while graduate students might not be their usual clientele, could offer some advice on certain kinds of positions. I would encourage you to think expansively about the kinds of experiences and advice that might help your students as they try to look for a broad range of careers. Your students will benefit from the effort, and they will likely be very grateful for the career support.
