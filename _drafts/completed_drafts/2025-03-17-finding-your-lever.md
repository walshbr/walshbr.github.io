What follows is my contribution[^1] to a roundtable at UVA entitled "[Digital Futures of Graduate Studies in the Humanities](https://cal.lib.virginia.edu/event/14161235)." The event celebrated the release of the [edited collection of the same name](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9781517916923/digital-futures-of-graduate-study-in-the-humanities/) distributed through the Debates in Digital Humanities series. As a part of the session, Gabriel Hankins, Alison Booth, and I gave remarks describing and reflecting on our published contributions to the volume.

---

![Slide depicting poster for Bill Murray comedy Groundhog Day](/assets/post-media/finding-your-lever/2.jpeg)

In the 1993 Bill Murray comedy, Groundhog Day, a weatherman gets trapped in a time loop, endlessly repeating the same day over and over again. It's often billed as a family friendly comedy, but I think it's actually quite dark. It's about the sort of the existential crisis that you feel when faced to relive the everyday over and over again, where your actions don't really matter, and it's a meditation on the kinds of small actions, small changes that you can make in a single day, whether they matter and ultimately, what your place is in the world. In a way, it's about the creeping horror of existing in the everyday when there is a larger, world-ending crisis going on that everyone is ignoring.

And frankly, the experience of Bill Murray in this movie of reliving the same thing over and over again is not at all dissimilar from my experience writing the piece that we're discussing today. 

![Slide depicting Dr. Manhattan meme - blue figure having the same thought repeatedly. It is X date and I am still thinking about the Trump administration's effects on DH Pedagogy](/assets/post-media/finding-your-lever/3.jpeg)

Put another way, let us transition from Murray to internet shit posting memes. In 2016 I was thinking about what the Trump administration means for teaching and learning. It's 2020 I was thinking about what the Trump administration means for teaching and learning. It's now 2024—I'm thinking about what the Trump administration means for teaching and learning. What are the effects of the recurrent crises that we are living through on higher education, graduate education in particular, on digital humanities. On those of us trying to teach, learn, and live in the academy, digitally or not. Be it about the Trump administration and their recent apocalyptic executive orders threatening to dismantle the financial infrastructure of the American university system as it has existed since the mid-twentieth century or about the full-on banning and crackdown on campus protests—we are in a state of extreme crisis But when has that not been true? 
 
![Slide depicting This is Fine Dog - a dog with a little hat in a burning building commenting "this is fine" as he blithely drinks coffee](/assets/post-media/finding-your-lever/4.jpeg)

To put it plainly, the house is on fire in academia. All too easy to remain focused on the small world in front of you, ignoring the actual burning building around you. To focus on the coffee. To go about business as usual, teaching within our disciplines, within our research areas, within our own band of digital humanities. 

This is all by way of framing the essay from the collection we are celebrating today. My contribution was entitled "The Futures of Digital Humanities Pedagogy in a Time of Crisis," and I will give a bit more context for those who haven't had time to read it. 

![Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@markuswinkler?utm_content=creditCopyText&utm_medium=referral&utm_source=unsplash">Markus Winkler</a> on <a href="https://unsplash.com/photos/white-and-black-braille-machine--TRcaFMV5vk?utm_content=creditCopyText&utm_medium=referral&utm_source=unsplash">Unsplash</a>](/assets/post-media/finding-your-lever/5.jpeg)

In case it's not clear, I was in a very angry space while writing this article, and its publication story was one of the more disorienting and time warping writing experiences I've ever had. If I recall correctly, I actually wrote off submitting to the deadline because I, frankly, felt overwhelmed. When finally I did so it was against the backdrop of the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic. I wrote the actual article in the summer of 2020 during the height of the Black Lives Matter protests related to the murder of George Floyd. The collection itself came out just after Trump's re-election to a second term. And of course this event is taking place just a couple months into the new administration.

I dwell on this long timeline, not unheard of in academic publishing, because of how dizzying it was to consistently feel as though I needed to reframe the crisis. At each stage, it what I had written in the past felt profoundly alien, and also immediately, urgently relevant to what I was currently feeling. Even though the specific context was constantly changing, I was writing to and for a digital humanities pedagogy framed in a higher education in crisis. But that feeling of crisis is not new. It is has always been there for people who are paying attention. The immediate characterization of the forces ranged against the week we do might change depending on the particular time of day, but they are always there. 

To summarize the argument of the article: we cannot have a neutral digital humanities pedagogy in this kind of climate. Because the climate is not neutral. Particular in the context of graduate education, our students are buffeted by systemic inequality, racism, a vanishing job market, and more. We do not teach in neutral spaces or in neutral times. It is not just morally and ethically necessary for us to develop a digital humanities pedagogy that engages with these kinds of crises. It's also a question of survival. It's clear more than ever that the world outside the classroom impacts the classroom itself, whether or not you want it do so. And the argument I make in the essay is that if we want to have a digital humanities and a digital humanities pedagogy that's worth having and that's worth surviving into the future it has to engage with the present. It has to reclaim it. 

I think the most recent and coordinated attacks from the Trump administration on funding and freedom of expression in higher education in America demonstrate this. The current environment, its far-right actors, and the administrators seeking to appease these forces for political expediency, are destabilizing trust, security, and the financial wellbeing of the infrastructure that makes teaching and learning possible. We cannot carry on with business as usual in the classroom. Because there very well could be no classroom. If we want to have a digital humanities pedagogy worth maintaining and worth surviving, it must take needs of its community seriously, the political urgencies of the moment seriously. 

![Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@florianolv?utm_content=creditCopyText&utm_medium=referral&utm_source=unsplash">Florian Olivo</a> on <a href="https://unsplash.com/photos/person-holding-black-lever-ca7tJ48YD64?utm_content=creditCopyText&utm_medium=referral&utm_source=unsplash">Unsplash</a>](/assets/post-media/finding-your-lever/6.jpeg)

I offer a number of ways in the piece for DH instructors to meet the moment, mostly by amplifying the work and actions of people who are already doing this labor and have been doing so for years. I name check everything from amplifying the voices of student protests, to joining your union, to giving space, funding, and support to scholars of color who have been doing this work for a long time. I suggest that we all look at the different spaces in which you have levers to pull, whether you're an administrator who has direct control over budgets that you can make more equitable, or if you're just a workaday classroom teacher who can engage your students in classroom assignments in the pursuit of justice: find ways to double down on your teaching as something that matters. 

I tried to do this work in the course of producing the essay itself, which I wrote differently than I usually do. While the piece does feel more argumentative than most of my writing, I started at the end. With the works cited. I worked from a process of accretion, accumulating a lot of relevant things and then relentlessly citing those people, projects, and organizations. The argument felt less like it emerged from my own voice out of it. More than anything else it felt like I was trying to channel a community of people doing the work who I admired as I tried to work through my own difficult feelings about the cultural moment. The full text of the actual essay is 2108 words. Of those, only 1210 words are the actual text. That means that 43% of the text is citations or footnotes. It felt a little wild to turn in. But I also think this sort of approach: radical solidarity and action across institutional, professional, and academic lines is the only way forward for digital humanities pedagogues. 

That's the piece, but I wanted to wind towards a close by talking a bit more about the editorial process. Being a piece in the Debates in Digital Humanities series, there were a number of phases of review. Gabe might be able to comment a little bit on what this collection was like to bring into being as an editor.

![Slide depicting two critiques, articulated in text following](/assets/post-media/finding-your-lever/7.jpeg)

I got two specific pieces of feedback that felt especially relevant to bring into discussion here: 

1. Would anyone disagree with what I'm saying? 
2. Am I arguing something that is specific to digital humanities pedagogy, or is this an argument about higher education more generally? 

I don't bring those up in a "can you believe what reviewer two said??" sense. Both these notes made the piece stronger. They also articulate reasonable objections to what I am discussing that are worth putting into the room. I enfolded responses into the essay itself, but I thought it worth sharing here as we move to discussion. 

![Slide depicting responses to these critiques, articulated in text following](/assets/post-media/finding-your-lever/8.jpeg)

As to whether anyone would disagree: in an abstract, values-oriented way, it might be the case that no one of a broad political inclination would disagree with what I was arguing. But, in fact, it is very difficult to convert that agreement into action. It is exceedingly difficult to convert temperament into the kind of work that could impact one's economic, social, or professional welfare. It's very hard to get people to have skin on the game. If you've ever tried to have an organizing conversation with someone about taking any kind of direct action, you know this to be true. If you've tried to get someone to join a union, that is not an easy conversation. In short, if you agree, what does it actually look like for you to take concrete pedagogical and political action with your agreement. With your teaching. With your administrating. What levers are you willing to pull? What are you not? How can you move a little more in the direction of exercising your own power, influence, and privilege?  

As to whether or not this argument is specific to the digital humanities or a broader argument about higher education: fair! After all, I did not talk that much about actual DH pedagogy here! In the piece itself I cite a range of DH work, but I think there is a way in which you could certainly make the argument discipline agnostic. I think my answer to the critique is yes. It can be both. Both broader than digital humanities pedagogy and specific in the way it manifests here.

The the kind of academic, class, and labor solidarity that I'm calling for is one that is wall to wall in the organizing sense. It should extend more broadly to include all of higher education, all of academia. I do believe that this particular political moment is an especially fraught one, and they way we get through it is by organizing together. By seeing our work, the work of education, as having a political valence to it beyond our particular methodological disciplinary silos. 

But! That is not to say that there is not also something distinct that digital humanities and digital humanities pedagogy can bring to the conversation. I believe that we have a unique perspective on the kinds of ways in which education intersects with the infrastructure the university. So much of the work that we do cuts across disciplinary, institutional, and administrative silos. How often does your digital humanities work require you to talk to IT professionals? To write grants? To talk to your librarians? To engage students in a kind of collaborative work that goes beyond the typical labor of the humanities classroom? It's often very hard for digital humanities teachers to conceive of their work as existing in isolation, closed within the walls of the classroom, simply because it is so often the work of many people from across the university. You have to intersect with policy decisions that are being made that affect the work that you're doing. So I think it is the case that digital humanities practitioners, scholars, and teachers, are especially well-positioned to frame their work as in dialog with political institutional decisions across the university, and to position their work as something that can enact the kind of change we want to see. There are plenty of examples of politically engaged digital humanities work that we can bring our DH teaching into conversation with. Maybe it's about organizing a data rescue event, collecting government data before it disappears as a vehicle for teaching data preservation. Maybe it's about using digital humanities budgets as a vehicle for teaching both DH administration and the political economy of the university? Maybe it's about articulating the equitable labor values you will practice as a part of your classroom DH projects? Fill in your own ideas.

![Photo by <a href="https://unsplash.com/@ninjason?utm_content=creditCopyText&utm_medium=referral&utm_source=unsplash">Jason Leung</a> on <a href="https://unsplash.com/photos/red-emergency-pull-lever-N2JUQtT5i40?utm_content=creditCopyText&utm_medium=referral&utm_source=unsplash">Unsplash</a>](/assets/post-media/finding-your-lever/9.jpeg)

In short, the article is a call for us to find your own levers, to find their own ways in which your digital humanities pedagogy can be politically impactful. Because it already subject to politics, already is political, whether you believe it or not. It's up to us to try and harness that. The spaces of teaching and learning are not closed to the outside world. This has always been true, but it's especially going to be true if the classroom disappears altogether. We better start acting like it before we have to start teaching in the streets. If we want to have a digital humanities in the future, if we want to have a digital future of graduate education, if we are to have any future at all, we have to start thinking about how our digital humanities pedagogy works on and in the present. 

Thank you.
