If you've ever hung out with me for more than a few seconds, you know that I'm obsessed with process. I'm always talking about some new thing that I'm trying. I'm going to do X thing over y number of days until I reach some milestone goal. Obviously not all of these can stick. Most of them don't. My latest goal has been to blog every week, I've been doing a pretty good job so far this semester. This impulse to write consistently is my own way to try and deal with the political climate we're living in. After all, if writing didn't matter, the powers that be wouldn't try so strongly to silence creativity disagreeing voices. Jeff Tweedy's _How to Write One Song_ has a great quote that has really stuck with me: "We have a choice— to be on the side of creation, or surrender to the powers that destroy." I've been trying to cultivate this practice of creation for myself. 

At the same time, lately I've also been trying to get back into exercise, something I have never been fairly attentive to when I was a kid, I was the least athletic person possible. Rather than running until I hurt myself, as I usually do when I try to get back into it, I'm working through a couch to 5k program. I'm honoring the real needs of my body, starting basically from nothing and building up to a healthier body. 

In that context, I've been thinking about writing like a muscle. How can I exercise those skills such that, when I sit down to write, I'm not waiting for inspiration to strike? What would it mean to build daily writing up in a sustainable practice? What would a writing program that asks you to write every day look like if it was modeled on a running program? My longterm goal was to spend some time working on a book project, something that often gets kicked to the back burner. And my means of getting there was to spend some time each day writing in whatever capacity I could.


In running, cliche that you only want to be working on either adding distance or increasing speed, try to double check that. In writing terms, I aimed to add a concrete number of words each day as opposed to writing for a specified amount of time. This meant that I could fit it into the cracks between things, typing on my phone or dictating while driving. I developed a plan for myself modeled on running programs that start out with small, set intervals for particular sessions: run for 15 seconds, walk for two minutes, run for 30 seconds, walk for two minutes. The proportions change and the amount increases week by week. 

Without further ado, here is the plan that I wrote and executed for myself in April: 

* Week 1:
  * Write one sentence every work day.
  * On weekends, write one sentence of creative work.
* Week 2:
  * Write two sentences every work day.
  * On weekends, write two sentences of creative work.
* Week 3:
  * Write three sentences every work day.
  * On weekends, write three sentences of creative work.
* Week 4:
  * Write four sentences every work day.
  * On weekends, write four sentences of creative work.

My goals were very modest starting out: just one one sentence that feels crafted. Each day, I will add pebble by pebble to my final product over the course of the month. This might feel ridiculous. What is the point of writing one sentence? How can you even get into that mindset? Fair critique. It depends on how you work. To help facilitate this, I treated it like an exercise requiring physical and mental warmup. For me, this most often meant that I would spend five minutes while driving just thinking and then ten minutes dictating. At first, I intentionally stopped myself after the goal number of sentences, doing no more than noting the upcoming topic for tomorrow's work. In this riff on Hemingway's advice to stop writing in the middle of a sentence, I tried to make sure that the next day's work would be ready to go. And the pivot to creative work on the weekends was a way to keep my momentum going while adopting a resting mentality, a way to tie in something enjoyable while still respecting work/life boundaries. 

I believe that it's possible to make inspiration strike for you, that it's better to work on a process oriented way in a craft mindset than to wait for huge chunks of time. After all those sentences will accumulate. During this process, I found that I was in a much better mood each day just knowing that I was making moves. By the end of the month I was writing nearly a full paragraph each day, and I abandoned my goal to stop writing after a certain point. I found that I kept finding new ways to fit writing in five minutes here, five minutes there. Slow progress still goes forward. Gotta start walking.
