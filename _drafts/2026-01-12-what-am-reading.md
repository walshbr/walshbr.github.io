As a child of the 90's, video games always felt like a race against the clock. I fell in love with roleplaying games for the narratives, but I only had so much time to play them before they had to be returned to our local rental store. Sending a game back to our local Blockbuster was always gamble. Sure, I might be able to rent it again later. But my save file could be gone, all evidence of my progress lost. I played for long hours, trying to see as much of the story as I could. 

On one trip to the store, I came across Breath of Fire II. The opening still takes my breath away. In it, you join your father and sister on a visit to a long-sleeping dragon who mysteriously appeared to defend your town the same night that your mother disappeared. As a player familiar with the series that features characters able to transform into dragons, it's easy to connect the dots and gather that you are communing with your sleeping mother. The game invites you to hang back alone and close your eyes to hear the dragon's voice. After doing so, you wake up to find that what remains of your family has disappeared and all of the NPCs you talked with mere moments ago act as though they have never met you. This act of forgetting renders the main character an orphan, leads to a life of suffering, and serves as a central mystery that drives the plot forward. 

Later in Breath of Fire's narrative, it becomes clear that a demon named Aruhamel is responsible for erasing the memories of everyone in your hometown. Defeating the monster is required to advance the plot, but doing so is fairly easy. Aruhamel barely attacks, instead inflicting status effects on your party (like amnesia) while you beat him up.

While I played Breath of Fire II as a child, my parents were in the midst of caring for my great aunt who was in the advanced stages of Alzheimer's disease. The disease is devastating. Watching someone you love slowly forget pieces of themselves and others is brutal.

Replaying games from my childhood has been a fascinating view into how things that change with distance. Those sprawling dungeons that seemed to take hours as a child turn out to be just a few rooms, easily navigated by an adult. An unstructured plot that leads you to wander an over world map turns out to be bread crumbed pretty significantly if you talk to the right NPC.[^1] The dialogue that I found captivating as a child can feel ham-fisted with the benefit of years.[^2] The focus on memory loss has only grown more powerful with time. 



It's become an Internet joke that JRPGS ask you to attend class on Monday and fight God on Friday. 

In the piece, I argue that the game elevates these human concerns by attributing memory loss to demon attacks while, at the same time, providing a vehicle to manage these traumatic experiences (those same demons can be defeated). In this way, retro gaming becomes as much about recovering narratives from aging media as it is about preserving the memory for the aging gamer that they ever took place at all.
