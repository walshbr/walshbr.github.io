Prompt 1: What are the three most important values that you bring to pedagogy? To digital pedagogy?

When I think about my teaching the first three words that come to mind are inclusion, community, comfort. Much of the reasons that those words stick out to me come from my own discomfort and anxiety that I felt in the classroom throughout college. I felt as though the pressure to speak, the pressure to make my voice heard for a participation grade, was suffocating. I remember watching the clock tick down and feeling a mounting sense of dread that I hadn't said a single thing in a particular session. Every time, without fail, this anxiety kept me from participating. Not once did the pressure to excel actually help me to do so. When I did find myself able to participate, it was universally confined to particular courses. I found myself so much more talkative in classes where I felt at ease. Rather than thinking of myself as a person who might not talk I just thought of myself as part of the community. 

Now that I'm on the other side of the classroom I find that I am very invested in those students who might feel unwelcome, those students who seem a bit more quiet than usual. This takes shape in all sorts of specific ways. I use alternative forms of grading that de-emphasize grades to encourage people to consider learning for their own sake. I keep an eye on who is not speaking and have a bag of tools I use to draw them in. I recognize and emphasize participation can take all sorts of forms. And even when it comes to digital technology, I tend to approach these tools and methods by emphasizing comfort first. Some find this approach lacking in rigor, as though it aims for something less than a stricter, hard line approach to the classroom. But, as Kevin Gannon says, "rigor is for corpses." Students can only be fully present in the classroom if they feel safe. All of my teaching is directed towards them feeling this way.

Prompt 2

How might you run this workshop in a way that reflects your approach to pedagogy? How will you teach digital methods in a way that reflects your pedagogy?

My values find their way into the DH classroom primarily in how I try to center the person. I focus on the anxieties that people bring to technology and to digital methods. I try to center the things they already know as opposed to what they don't. I de-emphasize the technology as a marker of expertise and, instead, encourage people to note the ways in which they already have command over things that are helpful to others. In this same way, I think that students, simply by being students, have so much that they can offer to other students. They are closer to learners than are those of us who have been doing this for a long time. That superpower fades over time—they should use it while they still have it. My approach to digital methods, then, is no different than how I approach other kinds of teaching. They are skills to be practiced and modeled for students. democratizing access to them is my first goal. 

This approach sometimes gets criticized for de-emphasizing actual expertise, but I always think that is fine. Those people comfortable seeking out expertise, who don't feel the ugly feelings I associate with learning digital methods, are likely to feel comfortable seeking out those experiences on their own. I am more worried about the people who might be left behind. Teaching to the whole class sometimes means that the people in the front won't need as much help as others. 

When I do my most satisfying teaching I find that it reflects these core values. The least satisfying are those moments of teaching where I find myself simply teaching to a tool or teaching a method to get through it. Those approaches to teaching have their place and their purpose, but they don't feel authentically me.

Prompt 3:

What activity are you planning for your workshop? If you don't have an activity yet, what are some materials you could use? How would you explain the concept or method in a way that a third-grader could follow you?

I'm skipping the main prompt here one because I am not teaching a specific topic or activity. But I am interested in how an elementary school audience can learn these materials. I often find some of the most interesting approaches to teaching are those with those audiences in mind. I'm thinking here of git for ages four up. That workshop uses tinker toys to explain version control, and I'm always impressed with just how expansive and inclusive the explanation is. The topic is very complicated and difficult for most newcomers, but keeping a childlike audience in mind helps to bring people along where they might otherwise feel left out. Watching my own toddler right now learn how to navigate the world, it's amazing how he can pick up just about anything if explained the right way, if given the right tools, metaphors, alternative ways of understanding. I've been told in the past that I am good at coming up with metaphors for digital topics, and I think it comes from this same place. If you have a particular topic that feels fixed in your mind for how confusing it is, it might be too rigid. Searching or alternative, simpler ways of describing it can help the topic to feel more open, more flexible, and easier to wrap your head around. It's a provocative statement, but I believe any topic, any technique, can be explained in a way that anyone can understand it. If a student has trouble with it, that's not the student's fault. It's the teachers. And now that I'm a parent I see a lot of resonance between my approach to teaching and my approach to teaching digital tools. It's all about patience, expansive curiosity, and the ways you can find your ways around mental blocks rather than accepting that they have to be there. If something seems impossible maybe just try thinking about it like a child would. 

Prompt 4

What are some challenges or issues that you anticipate? what about the workshop will cause you anxiety? What are those strategies or tactics that you can employ to alleviate these?

I think the biggest challenge to me in most of these teaching situations comes from how we tend to be asked to teach digital methods to one-off audiences. We don't know who is going to be in the room. We don't know what their backgrounds will be. We don't know what they will take away with them. In many ways, teaching a semester-long course with a stable set of units is teaching on easy mode. My approach to mitigate this problem has always been to push, instead, what I know people are likely to have. Give them a common foundation or approach in conversation that they can they take forward and practice in the workshop. So, rather than installing software ahead of time (but remaining unsure if participants will have similar technical backgrounds), I will introduce a foundational technique in the workshop itself. For sentiment analysis, I introduce students to how we might manually compute the sentiment of sentences by hand. For bag of words approaches to text, I tell students that counting words can be meaningful. For evaluating research projects, I suggest that a good research question comes from finding measurable evidence. Then the bulk of the workshop comes from practicing and discussing the thing I have taught them. I don't expect the participants to come into the workshop with any prior experience and, instead, I fill the cup and ask them to examine what is inside. The result, I hope, is that students find themselves a bit more on equal footing than they might otherwise be. This approach to teaching definitely does not go as far as, say, a workshop that uses Jupyter Notebooks to actually put a particular technique in students' hands. But I have always been uncomfortable with how those ways of teaching hand wave so much at what students need to know. If you only know the tip of the glacier can you you say you know how to move it? Or is a better approach to offer an ice cube that you can hold in your hand and turn over. Then, at least, a student might look at a glacier and have a deeper understanding of what ice is and can be. 

There I go with metaphors again.
